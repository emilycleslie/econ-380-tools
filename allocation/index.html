Who gets the apartments under a price ceiling

ECON 380 · Markets · Allocation mechanisms · About ten minutes

Jump to: Three possible allocation rules · 1 See it · 2 Summary of surplus and deadweight loss in each scenario · 3 Where the deadweight loss comes from · 4 Practice

The goal of this page is to illustrate how the allocation of available units under a price ceiling matters for efficiency.

We're continuing the apartment example from class: QD = 700 − P, QS = P, and rent capped at $200.

The question is which of the 500 people are going to get the 200 available apartments.

Read the demand curve as a spectrum of renters

Order the renters from the one who values an apartment most to the one who values it least. The horizontal axis is a renter's place on that spectrum. The height of the demand curve is that renter's willingness to pay (WTP): renter 300 values an apartment at 700 − 300 = $400.

The allocation rule determines which stretch of the demand curve is served. Consumer surplus is the area under that stretch and above $200.

Three possible allocation rules

With excess demand at the price ceiling, there are several mechanisms that could determine who gets the available units. (The real world may be messy and not fall cleanly into one of these.)

  • Highest WTP: renters 1–200 get them, the case from class. Mechanism: subletting. Anyone who values an apartment at less than $500 does better subletting it for $500, the rent at which demand is 200.
  • Lowest WTP: renters 301–500 get them. Mechanism: waiting in line. Apartments go to the renters with the lowest opportunity cost of waiting, who may also have the lowest WTP.
  • Random selection: 200 renters drawn at random from all 500. Mechanism: a housing lottery, like the ones some cities run for below-market apartments.

Served: renters 1–200, the high end of the spectrum, with WTP from $700 down to $500.

Consumer surplus

CS = ½(200)(700 − 500) + 200(500 − 200) = 80,000

A triangle on top of a rectangle.

Producer surplus

PS = ½(200)(200) = 20,000

The same under every rule.

Deadweight loss

DWL = 122,500 − (20,000 + 80,000) = 22,500

3 · Where the deadweight loss comes from

PS is the same under every rule, so the differences in deadweight loss all come from consumer surplus. A ceiling loses surplus in two ways.

Too few apartments. Landlords supply 200 instead of 350. That's the familiar triangle between demand and supply from 200 to 350: ½(350 − 200)(500 − 200) = 22,500. Every rule pays this.

The wrong renters may get them. If the 200 apartments don't go to the 200 renters who value them most, surplus is lost even on the apartments that are rented. The loss is the 80,000 the highest-WTP renters would have gotten, minus the CS this rule delivers.

Highest WTP: 22,500 + (80,000 − 80,000) = 22,500
Lowest WTP: 22,500 + (80,000 − 20,000) = 82,500
Random selection: 22,500 + (80,000 − 50,000) = 52,500

Same answers as section 2. This is why the deadweight loss of a price ceiling depends on more than supply and demand: it depends on how the units are allocated.

4 · Practice

Same market, but the ceiling is now $150. Work each one, then set the ceiling to $150 in the explorer to check.

  1. The lowest-WTP renters get the apartments. Which renters are served, and what are their WTPs?
    Answer

    Renters 401–550, WTP $300 down to $150.

    150 apartments are supplied and 550 renters want one, so the lowest-WTP 150 are renters 401–550. Their stretch of the demand curve starts at Q = 400, where WTP is 700 − 400 = $300.

  2. Still lowest WTP. What is consumer surplus?
    Answer

    ½(550 − 400)(300 − 150) = 11,250

    One triangle: base 150 apartments, height $300 − $150.

  3. Now the apartments are allocated by random selection. What is consumer surplus?
    Answer

    150550 × ½(550)(700 − 150) = 41,250

    Each of the 550 renters has a 150/550 chance. Multiply by the whole triangle above $150.

  4. Still random selection. What is deadweight loss?
    Answer

    122,500 − (11,250 + 41,250) = 70,000

    PS = ½(150)(150) = 11,250, under any rule.
