Marshallian demand → indirect utility

Definition

Marshallian demand gives the utility-maximizing bundle at prices PX and PY, with income I.

Indirect utility measures the utility from that bundle—the best utility the consumer can achieve.

XM(PX, PY, I),  YM(PX, PY, I)
V(PX, PY, I) = U(XM(PX, PY, I), YM(PX, PY, I))
Example with functional forms

Suppose U(X,Y) = √(XY), so the Marshallian demands are

XM(PX, PY, I) = I/(2PX)
YM(PX, PY, I) = I/(2PY)

Evaluate utility at that bundle:

V(PX, PY, I) = √((I/(2PX))(I/(2PY)))
  = I/(2√(PXPY))

Indirect utility → Marshallian demand

Roy's identity

Indirect utility tells us how the best attainable utility changes when prices or income change.

Compare the utility loss from a small price increase with the utility gain from a small income increase. The ratio tells us how much income would compensate for that price increase—which is the amount of the good the consumer buys.

XM(PX, PY, I) = -∂V(PX, PY, I)∂PX / ∂V(PX, PY, I)∂I
YM(PX, PY, I) = -∂V(PX, PY, I)∂PY / ∂V(PX, PY, I)∂I
Example with functional forms

From the previous panel, suppose

V(PX, PY, I) = I/(2√(PXPY))

Differentiate with respect to PX and I, then apply Roy's identity:

XM = --I/(4PX3/2√PY)1/(2√(PXPY))
  = I/(2PX)

This recovers the Marshallian demand from the indirect utility function.

Hicksian demand → Expenditure function

Hicksian demand gives the least-cost bundle that reaches utility U at prices PX and PY.

The expenditure function measures how much this bundle costs.

XH(PX, PY, U),  YH(PX, PY, U)
E(PX, PY, U) = PXXH(PX, PY, U) + PYYH(PX, PY, U)

Multiply each quantity by its price, then add the two costs.

Example with functional forms

For a Cobb–Douglas consumer, suppose the Hicksian demands are

XH(PX, PY, U) = U√(PY/PX)
YH(PX, PY, U) = U√(PX/PY)

Substitute these demands into the expenditure formula:

E = PXU√(PY/PX) + PYU√(PX/PY)
  = 2U√(PXPY)

Expenditure → Hicksian demand

Shephard's Lemma

Shephard's Lemma reverses the definition arrow: differentiate the expenditure function with respect to a good's price.

XH(PX, PY, U) = ∂E(PX, PY, U)∂PX
YH(PX, PY, U) = ∂E(PX, PY, U)∂PY

A tiny increase ΔPX raises minimum spending by approximately XHΔPX. The full lesson explains why re-optimization does not add a first-order term.

Indirect utility → expenditure

Invert with respect to income

Indirect utility starts with income and tells us the most utility attainable. Expenditure reads the same fact in reverse: it starts with a utility target and tells us the least spending required.

V(PX, PY, I) = U   ⇔   E(PX, PY, U) = I

Hold prices fixed and solve U = V(PX,PY,I) for I.

Example with functional forms

Suppose

U = I/(2√(PXPY))
I = 2U√(PXPY)

Therefore,

E(PX, PY, U) = 2U√(PXPY)

Expenditure → indirect utility

Invert with respect to utility

Expenditure starts with a utility target and tells us the least spending required. Indirect utility reads the same fact in reverse: it starts with income and tells us the most utility attainable.

E(PX, PY, U) = I   ⇔   V(PX, PY, I) = U

Hold prices fixed and solve I = E(PX,PY,U) for U.

Example with functional forms

Suppose

I = 2U√(PXPY)
U = I/(2√(PXPY))

Therefore,

V(PX, PY, I) = I/(2√(PXPY))

Marshallian demand ↔ Hicksian demand

Equivalence

These are two descriptions of the same choice when income and utility are matched.

XH(P,U) = XM(P,E(P,U))
YH(P,U) = YM(P,E(P,U))
XM(P,I) = XH(P,V(P,I))
YM(P,I) = YH(P,V(P,I))
