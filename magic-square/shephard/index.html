This is one arrow of the Magic Square: use the expenditure function to find Hicksian demand. Start with the intuition, then try the examples.

1 · Why it works

Start with something concrete. Imagine one specific good: gasoline.

Try it before looking at the calculation

Your friend drives to work every day, so assume their driving schedule does not change. Gas becomes $0.50 more expensive per gallon, and their monthly gas bill rises by $5.

How many gallons of gas do they buy each month?

gallons

What did we just calculate?

With the amount bought fixed, the number of gallons is the change in spending divided by the change in price.

The gas calculation

$5$0.50 per gallon = 10 gallons

A derivative is this same ratio for a tiny price change:

change in spendingchange in price  →  ∂E∂PX

For a fixed bundle (x0, y0), cost is

PXx0 + PYy0

Change PX, hold the bundle fixed, and the slope is x0: the number of X's.

Now allow re-optimization

So far we held driving fixed. Now relax that assumption. With a $0.50 increase, your friend might drive less. With a one-cent increase, they might adjust very little. A derivative focuses on this tiny-change case.

Find every place PX enters this expression. Click each one, then check.

E(, PY, Ū) = XH(, PY, Ū) + PYYH(, PY, Ū)

Apply the chain rule

∂E∂PX = XH + (PX∂XH∂PX + PY∂YH∂PX)
Direct effect: the price change applied to the old bundle.
Indirect effect / re-optimization: the cost effect of changing the bundle.

Why does Shephard's Lemma include only the first term? If PX rises, holding utility fixed usually means buying less X and more Y. The two indirect effects have opposite signs, so they offset each other.

In the derivative—the limit as the price change becomes tiny—they cancel exactly. For a small finite price change, the remaining effect of re-optimizing is small compared with the direct effect (second order).

If you have seen the envelope theorem in a math class, this is an envelope theorem result.

Shephard's Lemma

dE ≈ XHdPX

∂E∂PX = XH

What is held fixed?

Shephard's Lemma gives Hicksian demand. That means holding the utility target fixed, not income.

Hicksian

Hold Ū fixed; minimize spending.

Marshallian

Hold I fixed; maximize utility.

Common slip

Hicksian demand holds Ū fixed. Marshallian demand holds income I fixed.

2 · The rule

Differentiate the expenditure function with respect to a good's own price:

XH = ∂E∂PX
YH = ∂E∂PY

Hold the other price and Ū fixed. The result is Hicksian demand.

3 · Worked example

For U = X.5Y.5, take E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū.

Worked example

Differentiate with respect to PX:
XH = ∂E∂PX = 2 · (.5) PX−.5 PY.5 Ū
PY and Ū are constants.
= PX−.5 PY.5 Ū
The 2 and .5 cancel.
= (PYPX).5 Ū

This recovers the Hicksian demand from the expenditure function.

Common slip

Shephard's Lemma is one derivative. Roy's identity is a ratio with a minus sign.

Try it: find YH from the same E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū

YH = (PXPY).5 Ū

Same process: now differentiate with respect to PY.

4 · When the derivative is harder

Cobb-Douglas functions are easy. Here is one where a price appears twice.

Take as given the expenditure function E = PY(1 + Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX)).

Worked example

Find XH
∂E∂PX = PY · 1PX
PX appears once, inside the ln.
XH = PYPX

Common slip

PY appears twice, so use the product rule.

Try it: find YH from E = PY(1 + Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX))

YH = Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX)

Product rule: the 1 and −1 cancel.

5 · Practice

Differentiate each expenditure function.

  1. Given E = 4PX.25PY.75Ū, find XH.
    Answer

    XH = (PYPX).75 Ū

    4 · (.25) = 1.

  2. Given E = Ū(PX + PY), find XH.
    Answer

    XH = Ū

    No prices appear. These goods are bought in a fixed one-to-one bundle.

  3. Given E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū2, find YH.
    Answer

    YH = (PXPY).5 Ū2

    Ū2 is a constant because the derivative is with respect to price.

What this connects to

You now have one edge of the square: expenditure function → Hicksian demand. The other edges use the same logic.
