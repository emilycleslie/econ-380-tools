Expenditure function → Hicksian demand

ECON 380 · Magic Square · Shephard's lemma · About ten minutes

Jump to: 1 The rule · 2 Worked example · 3 Why it works · 4 When the derivative is harder · 5 Practice

This is one arrow of the Magic Square. In class we draw the square and talk about why the four objects connect at all. This page does the mechanics for one trip around it: you have the expenditure function, you want Hicksian demand. Read the rule, work the example, then do the practice problems.

1 · The rule

Hicksian demand is the derivative of the expenditure function with respect to that good's own price. That is the entire rule. There is no minus sign, nothing in a denominator, and nothing to solve for — you differentiate once and you are done.

XH = ∂E∂PX
YH = ∂E∂PY

Two things are being held fixed while you take that derivative: the other price, and Ū. Holding Ū fixed is not a simplification — it is what makes the answer Hicksian rather than Marshallian. Hicksian demand is the bundle that hits a fixed utility target as cheaply as possible, so a fixed target is exactly the right thing to be differentiating along.

In practice this means Ū behaves like any other constant in the derivative. It rides along untouched and shows up in your answer.

2 · Worked example

This is the function we use in class. Take as given that for U = X.5Y.5, the expenditure function is E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū.

Worked example

Find XH from E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū
XH = ∂E∂PX = 2 · (.5) PX−.5 PY.5 Ū
only PX moves — the 2, the PY.5 and the Ū are all constants riding along
= PX−.5 PY.5 Ū
the 2 and the .5 cancel
= (PYPX).5 Ū
a negative exponent on top is the same as a positive one underneath

That is the same XH we got the long way, by setting up the expenditure minimization problem and solving it. Which is the point of the square: once you have any one corner, you never have to set up a Lagrangian again.

Common slip

Importing the minus sign from Roy's identity. The two rules only look alike: Roy's is a ratio of two derivatives and carries a minus sign, while Shephard's is a single derivative with no minus sign and nothing underneath. If your Hicksian demand came out negative, this is almost always why.

Try it: find YH from the same E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū

YH = (PXPY).5 Ū

Same derivative, other letter. Now PX.5 is the constant and PY moves, so the prices trade places. That symmetry is a good sign you did it right — with these preferences the two goods are treated identically.

3 · Why it works

Taking a derivative to get a quantity looks like a coincidence the first time you see it. It isn't. Five short steps, and no step needs anything harder than multiplication.

Step 1 · What the expenditure function is asking

Only one question, and it is worth saying out loud: what is the cheapest bill that still reaches the utility target Ū?

Not what you happened to spend. The least you could spend and still get to Ū. Every number E reports is the winner of that competition at some set of prices.

Step 2 · Freeze one bundle and raise the price

Set optimizing aside for a moment. Pick one specific bundle — call it (x0, y0) — and just buy it, whatever happens. What it costs you is

cost of that fixed bundle = PXx0 + PYy0

Now let PX change while the bundle stays exactly where it is. Only the first term moves, and it moves in a straight line, so the slope of that cost with respect to PX is x0. In words:

Step 2, in one sentence

If the price of X rises by $1 per unit, the bill for this bundle rises by the number of units of X in the bundle.

Buying 10 units? A dollar on the price costs you $10. That is not calculus — it is multiplication, and it is already the whole shape of the answer.

Step 3 · Which bundle is the relevant one

Back to the real problem. At today's prices, the cheapest bundle that reaches Ū is the Hicksian bundle (XH, YH). That is the bundle E is buying right now.

So of all the fixed-bundle cost lines you could have drawn in Step 2, the one that matters at these prices is the one through the Hicksian bundle — and by Step 2, its slope is XH.

Step 4 · "But you'd rearrange the bundle"

Correct, and it is the right objection. When PX goes up, nobody is forced to keep buying the old bundle; you would shift a little toward Y and reach Ū more cheaply than the frozen bundle would.

That rearranging does save you something. The claim is only that it saves you very little — and the reason is Step 3. At the original prices you were already at the cheapest bundle that reaches Ū. Being at the cheapest point is exactly the situation where no small rearrangement pays off: if some shift toward Y had been worth making at those prices, you would already have made it. So when the price nudges, the best rearrangement available to you is worth very little next to the direct effect of paying more per unit.

For a very small price change, the direct effect is what matters. Not the only thing that happens — the only thing that matters to the first decimal places that count.

Step 5 · Put it together

For a very small change in PX, the change in the cheapest bill is approximately the Step 2 answer, using the Step 3 bundle:

dE ≈ XH dPX   for a small price change
∂E∂PX = XH   in the limit, exactly

The approximation in the first line is what a derivative is for. Shrink the price change toward zero and the leftover — the saving from rearranging — shrinks faster than the price change does, so it vanishes from the ratio. What survives is exact.

The same thing in dollars and cents

Numbers make the "saves you very little" claim checkable. Use the example from section 2: U = X.5Y.5 with Ū = 10, PY = 4 and PX = 4. The Hicksian bundle is (10, 10) and the bill is E = $80.

Now raise PX by one cent, to $4.01.

What the penny costs

Keep the same bundle: 10 units of X × $0.01 = $0.10 more
this is Step 2 — the number of units, times the price change
Re-optimize instead: E goes from $80.00000 to $80.09994
the new cheapest bundle is (9.9875, 10.0125) — it barely moved
So rearranging saved you $0.00006
about six thousandths of one cent, on a ten-cent change

The consumer really can re-optimize, and really does save something. It is six thousandths of a cent. The increase in expenditure is still, to any accuracy you care about, $0.10 — which is the 10 units of X in the bundle, times the penny.

The takeaway

A price derivative asks: if this price rises by one dollar per unit, how many extra dollars does the cheapest way of reaching the target require?

And the answer is: the number of units of that good in the Hicksian bundle. Which is why differentiating E hands you a quantity.

Utility is what's held fixed — not income

One thing to keep straight before the picture, because it is the difference between the two demand curves in this course.

Hicksian — this page

Held fixed: the utility target Ū. The consumer stays on one indifference curve no matter what prices do; the bill is whatever it has to be. Comes from minimizing spending subject to reaching Ū.

Marshallian — the other side

Held fixed: income I. The consumer spends the same money no matter what prices do; the utility reached is whatever it turns out to be. Comes from maximizing utility subject to a budget.

So there is no income anywhere in this derivation, and none in the answer. Watch for it in the first panel below: as you drag the price, the dot slides along one indifference curve and never leaves it. That is Ū being held fixed, drawn.

Technical note — the names for what just happened

Step 4 is the envelope theorem. The general statement: when you differentiate an optimized value with respect to a parameter, you may treat the choice variables as frozen at their optimal values, because the choices were already optimal and so contribute nothing at the margin. Step 2 is that "treat them as frozen"; Step 4 is the licence to do so.

There is also a picture behind it. Draw the fixed-bundle cost line from Step 2 for every bundle that reaches Ū, not just the current one. Each is a straight line in PX, and at any given price the cheapest bill is whichever line is lowest there. So E traces the bottom edge of that whole family — its lower envelope. A curve built as the bottom of a family of straight lines touches one of them at each price and takes that line's slope, which is Shephard's lemma again. This also explains why E is concave in prices.

The same argument on the utility-maximizing side of the square gives Roy's identity, and it is the reason the Lagrange multiplier means what it does.

  1. Drag the price all the way left. X is cheap relative to Y, so the cheapest way to reach utility 10 loads up on X, and XH is large. Notice the orange line goes steep. That is not a coincidence: if you own a lot of units of X, then every dollar added to PX hits all of them, so your bill climbs fast. Many units, steep line — the same fact said two ways.
  2. Now all the way right. The bundle slides around the curve toward Y, XH drops, and the orange line flattens — when you're barely buying any X, a rise in its price hardly moves your bill at all. Watch the dot in the bottom panel while you drag: you are drawing a Hicksian demand curve.
  3. Watch the top two numbers in the box. They answer different questions — "how fast is the bill rising?" and "how many units of X am I buying?" — and they never once disagree. That is Shephard's lemma, and there is nothing else to it.
  4. Now the penny, at the bottom of the box. At any price you like, compare what a one-cent rise costs you if you keep this bundle against what it costs after you re-optimize. The second number is smaller — rearranging genuinely does save something — but look at how much smaller. That gap is all of Step 4, in dollars, and it is what "for a very small price change" buys you.
  5. The subtle one. The orange line touches E at today's price and sits above it everywhere else — of course it does, since at any other price you would rather re-optimize than keep this bundle. But look how slowly it pulls away near the touching point. Right next to the dot the line and the curve are nearly the same thing, and that is exactly why the slope of the curve equals the slope of the line.

Common slip

Reading the third panel as an ordinary demand curve and expecting it to shift when income changes. It won't — there is no income in it. Look at the axes of the first panel as you drag: the consumer stays pinned to the same indifference curve the whole time. Ū is what's held fixed here, not I, and that is the entire difference between this curve and the Marshallian one.

4 · When the derivative is harder

Every Cobb-Douglas expenditure function is a product of powers, so the derivative is one line. Other expenditure functions are not, and then the only difficulty is calculus you already have. Here is one where the price appears twice.

Take as given the expenditure function E = PY(1 + Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX)).

Worked example

Find XH
∂E∂PX = PY · 1PX
PX appears in one place only, inside the ln — no product rule needed
XH = PYPX

Common slip

Going after YH the same way. PY shows up twice in that expenditure function — once out front and once inside the log — so that one needs the product rule. Before you differentiate, count how many times the price you're differentiating with respect to actually appears.

Try it: find YH from E = PY(1 + Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX))

YH = Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX)

Product rule. The first piece is the bracket itself: (1 + Ū − ln(PY) + ln(PX)). The second is PY times the derivative of the bracket, which is PY · (−1/PY) = −1. Add them and the 1 and the −1 cancel.

5 · Practice

Three problems. Each one hands you an expenditure function and asks for one Hicksian demand — no setup, no solving, just the derivative.

  1. Given E = 4PX.25PY.75Ū, find XH.
    Answer

    XH = (PYPX).75 Ū

    4 · (.25) = 1, leaving PX−.75PY.75Ū. The constants cancelling to 1 is not a coincidence — it happens for every Cobb-Douglas expenditure function.

  2. Given E = Ū(PX + PY), find XH.
    Answer

    XH = Ū

    No prices in the answer at all. That is correct, and it tells you what these preferences are: the consumer buys the two goods in a fixed one-to-one bundle and never substitutes, no matter what the prices do. A relative price change moves the bill but not the bundle.

  3. Given E = 2PX.5PY.5Ū2, find YH.
    Answer

    YH = (PXPY).5 Ū2

    The Ū2 is there to make a point: you are not differentiating with respect to Ū, so whatever it looks like, it is a constant. Copy it into the answer and move on.

What this connects to

You now have one edge of the square. From XH the next step is usually up the left edge to Marshallian demand, or back around to V — and on an exam, "go all the way around the magic square" means chaining three of these in a row. Each one is a single move like this one.
