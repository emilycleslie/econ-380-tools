Indirect utility → Marshallian demand

ECON 380 · Magic Square · Roy's identity · About ten minutes

This is one arrow of the Magic Square: use indirect utility to recover Marshallian demand. Start with the intuition, then apply Roy's identity.

1 · Why it works

Suppose the price of X rises. The consumer's best attainable utility falls. More income would push utility back up.

Try it before looking at the calculation

Suppose the price of gas rises by $0.10 per gallon. Your friend says that if she had $0.80 more per month, she wouldn't care about the increase in the price of gas at all.

How many gallons per month would you estimate she was buying?

gallons per month

The compensation calculation

$0.80 more per month$0.10 more per gallon = 8 gallons per month

If your friend buys 8 gallons, a $0.10 increase per gallon costs her $0.80 more per month. That is the extra income she says would make her just as well off as before.

The 10 cents is the small price change dPX. The 80 cents is the compensating income change dI. Their ratio, dI/dPX, measures how much extra income is needed per unit of the price increase.

Shephard's Lemma identifies this slope

Let U0 = V(P,I) be the consumer's current utility. Holding U0 fixed, the income she needs is expenditure E(P,U0). Therefore,

dIdPX = ∂E∂PX = XH(P,U0)

At these matched prices and utility, Hicksian and Marshallian demand select the same bundle:

XH(P,U0) = XM(P,I)

So the 8 gallons from the compensation calculation is the consumer's Marshallian demand at the original prices and income.

Review earlier stops

Why is compensating income expenditure? The expenditure function gives the minimum income needed to reach a fixed utility level. Thus, when utility is held at U0, the required income is I = E(P,U0).

Why do the two demands match here? At the original prices, E(P,U0) = I. The expenditure-minimizing and utility-maximizing problems therefore select the same bundle.

Review Shephard's Lemma · Review Marshallian–Hicksian equivalence

Roy's identity calculates the same compensation slope using indirect utility. A small price change and a small compensating income change leave utility unchanged:

dU = ∂V∂PXdPX + ∂V∂IdI = 0

The price effect is negative and the income effect is positive. Divide by dPX and solve for the same dI/dPX that appeared in the gas example:

0 = ∂V∂PX + ∂V∂I dIdPX
dIdPX = -∂V/∂PX∂V/∂I

The left side is the compensation slope that Shephard's Lemma identifies as XH, and matched Hicksian demand equals XM. Therefore:

Roy's identity

XM(PX, PY, I) = -∂V/∂PX∂V/∂I
YM(PX, PY, I) = -∂V/∂PY∂V/∂I

The extra income is a compensation thought experiment; Marshallian demand itself still holds actual income fixed.

2 · Another way: the Lagrangian and the envelope theorem

Write the utility-maximization problem as a Lagrangian:

ℒ = U(X,Y) + λ[I - PXX - PYY]

At the utility-maximizing bundle, the consumer uses the whole budget. The constraint term is therefore zero:

ℒ* = U(X*,Y*) V(PX,PY,I) + λ* [I - PXX* - PYY*] = 0 = V(PX,PY,I)

The optimal quantities are the Marshallian demands. In particular, X* = XM(PX, PY,I).

Now change a price or income by a tiny amount. The envelope theorem says that the first-order change in utility comes from the explicit terms in the Lagrangian. We do not need to track the small movement in the optimal bundle. In other words, we do not add chain-rule terms involving dX*/dPX or dY*/dPX; those first-order terms cancel at the optimum.

∂V∂I = ∂ℒ∂I = λ*
∂V∂PX = ∂ℒ∂PX = -λ*XM

One more dollar raises utility by λ*. A higher price for X lowers utility by λ* for every unit of X purchased.

Now divide the price derivative by the income derivative. Both contain the same λ*, so the multiplier cancels. The price derivative already contains a minus sign, so put another minus sign in front of the ratio:

-∂V/∂PX∂V/∂I = --λ*XMλ* = XM

This reaches the same identity directly from utility maximization, without going through the expenditure function or Hicksian demand.

3 · Worked example

Suppose we start with the indirect utility function

V(PX, PY, I) = I/(2√(PXPY))

Now differentiate:

∂V∂PX = -I/(4PX3/2√PY)
∂V∂I = 1/(2√(PXPY))

Roy's identity recovers Marshallian demand from the indirect utility function:

XM = --I/(4PX3/2√PY)1/(2√(PXPY))
  = I4PX3/2√PY × 2√(PXPY)
  = I/(2PX)

This is the familiar equal-share Cobb–Douglas demand: the consumer spends half of income on X. For example, the indirect utility function above is generated by U(X,Y) = √(XY).

The takeaway

Roy's identity turns the effects of prices and income on indirect utility back into the quantities in the utility-maximizing bundle.
