Exponents, logs, and simplifying expressions

ECON 380 · Math Proficiency 1 · About ten minutes

Jump to: 1 Exponent rules · 2 Fractional and negative exponents · 3 Natural logs · 4 Simplifying a ratio · 5 Quotients as products

This page covers the content you need to pass Math Proficiency 1. Read the five rules, work the five practice problems, then retake the exam. That's the whole assignment. The section numbers match the exam's question groups, so if you missed a question about logs, section 3 is the one to read.

1 · Exponent rules Exam group 1

Every exponent question in this course comes down to these three. Notice that multiplying expressions adds exponents, while raising a power to a power multiplies them — mixing those two up is the single most common error.

xa xb = xa + b
(xa)b = xab
xaxb = xa − b

Worked example

Simplify (x2y4)2 · x
= x4y8 · x
the outer 2 multiplies both exponents
= x5y8
then the loose x adds 1 to the x exponent only

Common slip

The loose x at the end only touches the x exponent. It never touches y. Different bases never combine.

Try it: simplify (x3y2)2 · x4

x10y4

The outer 2 gives x6y4, then x6 · x4 = x10.

2 · Fractional and negative exponents Exam group 2

A root is a fractional exponent, and that's all it is. Once you write every root as a power, the rules from section 1 do all the work — you never need a separate set of rules for square roots. A negative exponent is likewise just division, and it is a perfectly good final answer. You do not have to convert it back into a fraction.

√x = x1/2
x−n = 1xn
x = x1

Worked example

Simplify (x3 y x4)1/2
= (x7y)1/2
collect the x's first — inside the parentheses, exponents add
= x7/2 y1/2
the 1/2 multiplies every exponent, including y's invisible 1

Common slip

Forgetting that y is really y1, and leaving it as y instead of y1/2. If a variable has no exponent written, write the 1 in before you start.

Try it: simplify (x2 y x)1/2

x3/2 y1/2

Inside: x2 · x1 = x3. Then halve both exponents.

3 · Natural logs Exam group 3

Logs turn multiplication into addition. That is the whole reason we use them. When a utility function is a product of powers — which most of ours will be — taking logs turns it into something you can differentiate one term at a time. Two rules do the work, and the third just says that e and ln undo each other.

ln(xy) = ln(x) + ln(y)
ln(xa) = a ln(x)
ln(xy) = ln(x) − ln(y)
ln(ex) = x   and   eln(x) = x

Worked example

Let u = xαyβ. Find ln(u).
ln(u) = ln(xα) + ln(yβ)
the product splits into a sum first
= α ln(x) + β ln(y)
then each exponent comes out front

Common slip

There is no rule for ln(x + y). Logs split products and quotients, never sums. If you see a plus sign inside a log, you are probably stuck on purpose — leave it alone.

Try it: simplify ln(x5 / y3)

5 ln(x) − 3 ln(y)

The division becomes subtraction of two logs — not division of two logs.

4 · Simplifying a ratio Exam group 4

All semester you will divide one messy expression by another and need the answer to collapse into something short. It always works the same way: take one base at a time and subtract exponents. Don't try to see the whole thing at once.

Worked example

Simplify α Xα−1 Yββ Xα Yβ−1
the constants: α over β, and they stay put
X: Xα−1 / Xα = X−1
(α − 1) − α = −1
Y: Yβ / Yβ−1 = Y1
β − (β − 1) = 1
= αβ X−1 Y = α Yβ X

If that looks like a lot of machinery for one answer, here's the payoff: the expression you just simplified is a marginal rate of substitution for Cobb-Douglas preferences. You will compute it in Lecture 2 and then again, in one form or another, for the rest of the semester.

Common slip

Cancelling the variables entirely and answering α/β. The exponents differ by one, so the variables don't fully cancel — one Y survives on top and one X survives underneath.

Try it: simplify 5X4Y3 / (3X5Y2)

5Y3X

X: 4 − 5 = −1, so X goes underneath. Y: 3 − 2 = 1, so Y stays on top. The 5 and the 3 don't cancel.

5 · Quotients as products Exam group 5

Whenever you meet a fraction you have to differentiate, rewrite it as a product first using a negative exponent. Then the product rule handles it. This is a deliberate choice for this course: one less rule to memorize, and one less place to make a sign error. You will not need the quotient rule this semester.

fyb = f · y−b

Worked example

Rewrite 2x4y3 as a product
= 2x4y−3
dividing by y3 is multiplying by y−3

Common slip

Subtracting the exponents across different bases and answering 2x. The x and the y are different bases — their exponents never interact.

Try it: rewrite 5x4 / y2 as a product

5x4y−2

Flip the sign on the exponent of whatever was in the denominator, and drop the fraction bar.
