Derivatives and critical points

ECON 380 · Math Proficiency 2 · About ten minutes

Jump to: 1 The power rule · 2 The product rule · 3 The chain rule · 4 Derivatives of ln and e · 5 Critical points

This page covers the content you need to pass Math Proficiency 2. Read the five rules, work the five practice problems, then retake the exam. That's the whole assignment. The section numbers match the exam's question groups, so if you missed a question about the chain rule, section 3 is the one to read.

1 · The power rule Exam group 1

This is the one you'll use most often. Multiply by the exponent, then knock the exponent down by one. It works for every exponent — positive, negative, or fractional — with no special cases.

y = Axn  →  dydx = Anxn − 1

Worked example

y = 3x4
dydx = 3 · 4x3 = 12x3
multiply by the exponent, then drop it by one

Worked example, negative exponent

y = 5x−2
dydx = 5 · (−2)x−3 = −10x−3
−2 − 1 = −3, not −1

Common slip

Subtracting one from a negative exponent makes it more negative. x−2 becomes x−3, not x−1. Say the arithmetic out loud if you have to.

Try it: find dy/dx for y = 4x−3

−12x−4

4 · (−3) = −12, and −3 − 1 = −4.

2 · The product rule Exam group 2

When two things multiply, you can't just differentiate each and multiply the results. Differentiate one at a time and add.

(uv)' = u'v + uv'

Worked example

y = x2 ln(x4)
= 4x2 ln(x)
simplify inside the log first — it saves you the whole mess
u = x2,  u' = 2x     v = 4ln(x),  v' = 4x
dydx = 2x · 4ln(x) + x2 · 4x = 8x ln(x) + 4x

Common slip

Answering u'v' — differentiating both and multiplying. That is not the product rule and it gives the wrong answer every time. Also: always simplify inside a log before you differentiate.

Try it: find dy/dx for y = (2x + 1)(3x + 4)

12x + 11

2(3x + 4) + 3(2x + 1) = 6x + 8 + 6x + 3. Both pieces contribute an x term, which is why the coefficient doubles.

3 · The chain rule Exam group 3

When something is tucked inside something else, differentiate the outside, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Forgetting that second step is the single most common calculus error in this course.

y = f(g(x))  →  dydx = f'(g(x)) · g'(x)

Worked example

y = (3x + 2)5
outside: 5(3x + 2)4
inside: 3x + 2 differentiates to 3
dydx = 15(3x + 2)4

Common slip

Stopping at 5(3x + 2)4 and forgetting to multiply by the 3. Whenever the thing inside the parentheses is anything other than a bare x, there's a second factor coming.

Try it: find dy/dx for y = (2x2 + 5)3

12x(2x2 + 5)2

Outside gives 3(2x2 + 5)2; the inside differentiates to 4x. Multiply: 3 · 4 = 12.

4 · Derivatives of ln and e Exam group 4

Two rules, and both get the chain rule applied on top when there's something other than a bare x inside.

y = ln(x)  →  dydx = 1x
y = ex  →  dydx = ex
y = eu  →  dydx = u'eu

Worked example

y = ln(5x3)
= ln(5) + 3ln(x)
split it with the log rules from Math Proficiency 1
dydx = 3x
ln(5) is a constant, so it differentiates to zero

Common slip

Keeping the 5. Any constant multiplying inside a log becomes an additive constant once you split the log, and constants differentiate to zero — so it disappears completely. The answer doesn't depend on it at all.

Try it: find dy/dx for y = e4x

4e4x

The e part never changes. The inside, 4x, differentiates to 4, and that comes out front.

5 · Critical points Exam group 5

Every optimization in this course — utility maximization, cost minimization, profit maximization — is this procedure with economics attached. Two steps: find where the first derivative is zero, then use the second derivative to find out what kind of point you landed on.

f'(x) = 0   →   a critical point
f''(x) < 0   →   a maximum
f''(x) > 0   →   a minimum
f''(x) = 0   →   an inflection point

Worked example

f(x) = 2x3 − 6x
f'(x) = 6x2 − 6 = 0  →  x2 = 1  →  x = 1 or x = −1
two critical points, and they are not the same kind
f''(x) = 12x
at x = 1:  f'' = 12 > 0, a minimum
at x = −1: f'' = −12 < 0, a maximum

Common slip

Stopping once you've solved f'(x) = 0. Finding the critical point is only half the job — until you check the second derivative you don't know whether you've found the best outcome or the worst one.

Try it: find and classify the critical point of f(x) = ln(2x2) − x

x = 2, a maximum

Split the log: f = ln(2) + 2ln(x) − x, so f' = 2/x − 1 = 0 gives x = 2. Then f'' = −2/x2, which is negative for any positive x — so it's a maximum.
