Partial derivatives

ECON 380 · Math Proficiency 3 · About fifteen minutes

This page covers the content you need to pass Math Proficiency 3. Read the five rules, work the practice problems, then retake the exam. The section numbers match the exam's question groups, so if you missed a question about logs, section 4 is the one to read.

If you have never seen a partial derivative before, you are not behind. Plenty of people arrive in this course without having met them, and they are much less new than they look. Every derivative rule you already know still applies, unchanged. There is exactly one thing added, and section 1 is entirely about that one thing. Take this page slowly and you will be fine — we use partial derivatives constantly, so it is worth the extra ten minutes now.

1 · Holding the other variable constant Exam group 1

A partial derivative is an ordinary derivative with one extra instruction: every variable except the one you are differentiating is treated as a number. That is the whole idea. Nothing else changes.

∂f∂x means: differentiate with respect to x, treat y as a constant
∂f∂y means: differentiate with respect to y, treat x as a constant

The curly ∂ instead of a straight d is just a signal that the function has more than one variable and you are only moving one of them. You may also see the same thing written fx or f1 — all three mean the same.

Here is what that looks like. A function of two variables is a surface rather than a curve. Pin y at some value and the surface becomes an ordinary curve, which has an ordinary slope:

You are not stuck with the value you pinned. Pin y somewhere else and you get a different curve with a different slope — and the formula you get for ∂f/∂x covers every one of them at once. That is why the answer often still has y in it.

Why y sometimes disappears and sometimes doesn't

This is the part that trips almost everyone, and it is worth getting straight before you go further. Whether y survives depends entirely on whether it is added or multiplied.

Added

f = 3x2 + 5y3

∂f∂x = 6x

The 5y3 is a separate term. It doesn't move when x moves, so it adds nothing to the rate of change. It's gone.

Multiplied

f = 3x2y3

∂f∂x = 6xy3

The y3 multiplies the whole thing. It scales the answer, exactly the way the 3 does. It stays.

The test that always works

Pretend y is just the number 8, and watch what happens.

f = 3x2 + 8  →  f' = 6x   — the 8 vanished
f = 3x2 · 8 = 24x2  →  f' = 48x = 6x · 8   — the 8 stayed

Adding a constant shifts a function up or down; it doesn't change any slope, so it contributes nothing to the derivative. Multiplying by a constant stretches the whole function, so it stretches the slope too. y behaves exactly the same way — because while you are differentiating with respect to x, that is precisely what it is: a constant.

Worked example, with both kinds of term

f(x,y) = 2x4 + 2y4 + 4xy
∂f∂x = 8x3 + 4y
2y4 is added on and has no x in it, so it is gone
4xy has y multiplied in, so the y stays as a coefficient

Common slip

The two mistakes are mirror images, and people often make both. Keeping the added term, and answering 8x3 + 8y3 + 4y. Or dropping the multiplied one, and answering 8x3 + 4. If you are unsure which case you are in, replace y with 8 and look again.

Try it: find ∂f/∂y for f = 3x2 + 5y3 + 2xy

15y2 + 2x

The 3x2 is added on with no y in it, so it goes. The 5y3 differentiates normally. The 2xy has x multiplied in, so the x stays.

2 · Cobb-Douglas partials Exam group 2

You'll see a lot of this one, because it's where marginal utility comes from in one of the most commonly used functional forms in economic theory. Bring the exponent of your variable down in front, reduce that exponent by one, and leave the other variable's power completely alone.

Worked example

f(x,y) = 7x5y4
∂f∂x = 35x4y4
7 · 5 = 35, and the x exponent drops from 5 to 4
the y4 is multiplied in, so it rides along untouched

Worked example, with symbols

f(x,y) = xαyβ
∂f∂x = αxα−1yβ
identical rule; the symbols change nothing

That last one is worth recognizing on sight. It's the marginal utility of a Cobb-Douglas utility function, and when you divide one of these by the other — using the ratio move from Math Proficiency 1 — you get the MRS.

Common slip

Dropping the other variable, and answering 35x4. Everything here is multiplied together, so nothing disappears — the y4 is being held constant, not deleted.

Try it: find ∂f/∂y for f = 4x3y2

8x3y

4 · 2 = 8, the y exponent drops from 2 to 1, and x3 comes along unchanged.

3 · Partials involving e Exam group 3

The chain rule from Math Proficiency 2, with the same one extra instruction. Here u(x,y) stands for whatever function sits up in the exponent — it is a function, not a variable, and it usually has both x and y in it.

f = eu(x,y)  →  ∂f∂x = ∂u(x,y)∂x · eu(x,y)

Worked example

f(x,y) = 2xy + e4xy
here u(x,y) = 4xy
first term: 2xy → 2y
∂u(x,y)∂x = 4y
differentiating 4xy with respect to x leaves the y behind
∂f∂x = 2y + 4ye4xy

Common slip

Leaving the exponent's derivative off and answering 2y + e4xy. The e part never changes, but the factor out front is the whole point of the chain rule.

Try it: find ∂f/∂y for f = e3xy

3xe3xy

Here u(x,y) = 3xy, and ∂u/∂y = 3x. That multiplies the unchanged e3xy.

4 · Partials involving ln Exam group 4

It's not necessary, but it can be helpful to split the log first, using the rules from Math Proficiency 1. Once it's a sum, most of it turns out to be constants and the problem collapses.

Worked example

f(x,y) = ln(x3y5)
= 3 ln(x) + 5 ln(y)
splitting it turns one messy log into two easy terms
∂f∂x = 3x
5 ln(y) is constant with respect to x, and it is added on, so it drops out

Common slip

Going straight for 1/u and answering 1/(x3y5). That's the chain rule half-done — you still owe the derivative of the inside. Splitting the log first avoids the problem completely, and it's less work.

Try it: find ∂f/∂y for f = 4 ln(xy) · z

4zy

Split: 4z ln(x) + 4z ln(y). The first term is constant with respect to y and is added on, so it goes. In the second, 4z is multiplied in, so it stays — giving 4z/y.

5 · Second partials Exam group 5

Do it twice. Differentiate with respect to the first variable, then differentiate that answer with respect to the second. There's no shortcut and no new rule — just don't stop after the first step.

∂2f∂x∂y  =  differentiate by x, then differentiate the result by y

Worked example

f(x,y) = 4x2y3 + ln(x)y
by x:   8xy3 + yx
ln(x) differentiates to 1/x, and the y is multiplied in, so it stays
then by y:   24xy2 + 1x
y/x differentiates to 1/x, because 1/x is multiplied in

Common slip

Stopping after the first derivative, or losing the log term along the way. It doesn't vanish — y/x still has a y in it, so it survives the second step and becomes 1/x.

Try it: find ∂2f/∂x∂y for f = 3x2y4

24xy3

By x first: 6xy4. Then by y: 24xy3. Both exponents end up one lower than they started.

More practice

Work these on paper before you open the answers. If you only do some of them, do number 1 — the whole additive-versus-multiplied distinction is in that one pair.

  1. These two look almost identical. Find ∂f/∂x for each.
    (a) f = x2 + y5     (b) f = x2y5
    Answer

    (a) 2x     (b) 2xy5

    In (a) the y5 is added on, so it contributes nothing to how f changes with x. In (b) it multiplies, so it scales the answer. Same symbols, opposite fates.

  2. Find ∂f/∂x for f = 4x3 + 7y2 − 2xy
    Answer

    12x2 − 2y

    The 7y2 is added on and goes; the −2xy keeps its y.

  3. Same function — now find ∂f/∂y for f = 4x3 + 7y2 − 2xy
    Answer

    14y − 2x

    Now it's 4x3 that disappears, and the cross term keeps its x instead.

  4. Find ∂f/∂y for f = 5x2y4
    Answer

    20x2y3

    5 · 4 = 20, the y exponent drops to 3, and x2 is untouched.

  5. Find ∂f/∂y for f = xαyβ
    Answer

    βxαyβ−1

    This is a marginal utility. You will write it many times this semester.

  6. Find ∂f/∂x for f = e5xy
    Answer

    5ye5xy

    u(x,y) = 5xy, so ∂u/∂x = 5y.

  7. Find ∂f/∂y for f = 2xy + e3xy
    Answer

    2x + 3xe3xy

    Both terms keep an x, because in both it is multiplied in rather than added on.

  8. Find ∂f/∂y for f = ln(x4y2)
    Answer

    2y

    Split it: 4 ln(x) + 2 ln(y). The first term is constant with respect to y and added on, so it goes.

  9. Find ∂2f/∂x∂y for f = 6x3y2 + ln(x)y
    Answer

    36x2y + 1x

    By x first: 18x2y2 + y/x. Then by y: 36x2y + 1/x.

That's all of it

If the practice problems went smoothly, go retake the exam now. It pulls eight questions at random, so you'll get a different mix each time. You need seven of the eight.

If they didn't, the section to reread is almost always section 1 — nearly every mistake on this material traces back to whether the other variable was added or multiplied.
