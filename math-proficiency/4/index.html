Total differentials and total derivatives

ECON 380 · Math Proficiency 4 · About ten minutes

Jump to: 1 Writing a total differential · 2 Through a common variable · 3 When y depends on x · 4 Level curves

This page covers the content you need to pass Math Proficiency 4. Read the five rules, work the five practice problems, then retake the exam. The section numbers match the exam's question groups.

There is almost nothing new to compute here. Everything on this page is built from partial derivatives you already know how to take.

1 · Writing a total differential Exam group 1

A partial derivative tells you what happens if one variable moves. The total differential tells you what happens if both move at once: each variable contributes its own partial times its own small change, and you add the contributions up.

df = ∂f∂x dx + ∂f∂y dy

What it actually means

Say f measures your altitude on a mountainside, x is how far you walk east, and y is how far you walk north. You take one small step — a little to the east, a little to the north. How much does your altitude change?

Two things matter in each direction: how far you went, and how steep it is that way. The steepness heading east is ∂f/∂x, and how far east you went is dx — multiply them and you get the altitude you gained from the eastward part of your step. Do the same going north. Add the two, and that is your total change in altitude.

That is the whole content of the formula. Each direction contributes its own steepness times its own distance, and the contributions add up. It's an approximation, and it gets better the smaller the step — which is why we write dx rather than a big change in x.

Worked example

f(x,y) = 4x2y
∂f/∂x = 8xy     ∂f/∂y = 4x2
df = 8xy dx + 4x2 dy
that's the whole thing — take both partials, then write them down together

Common slip

Putting the coefficients on the wrong differentials. The partial with respect to x goes with dx, always. If you find x's partial sitting next to dy, they've been swapped.

Try it: find df for f = 3x3y2

9x2y2 dx + 6x3y dy

Both partials, each paired with its own differential.

2 · Total derivatives through a common variable Exam group 2

Sometimes x and y both depend on some third thing — t, say. Then f really depends only on t, and you can ask how it changes as t moves. Each variable is a separate channel, and you add the channels up.

dfdt = ∂f∂x dxdt + ∂f∂y dydt

Worked example

f = x2y,   x = 2t,   y = t3
2xy · 2  +  x2 · 3t2
= 4xy + 3x2t2 = 8t4 + 12t4 = 20t4
check it the other way: f = (2t)2t3 = 4t5, which differentiates to 20t4

That check is worth knowing. When you can substitute first and differentiate once, do it — it's usually quicker and it gives you a way to verify the formula answer.

Common slip

Using only one channel. Both variables move when t moves, so both contribute, and leaving one out gives an answer that is too small.

Try it: find df/dt for f = 3x + 4y, where x = t2 and y = 2t

6t + 8

3 · 2t + 4 · 2. The second term has no t left, because dy/dt = 2 is just a number.

3 · Total derivatives when y depends on x Exam group 3

Now the awkward case: y is itself a function of x. Changing x then affects f twice — directly, and again by dragging y along with it.

dfdx = ∂f∂x + ∂f∂y dydx

Note the two different ds in that line. The straight df/dx on the left is the whole effect; the curly ∂f/∂x on the right is only the direct part. They are not the same thing, and the difference is the entire point.

Worked example

f = x2y,   with y = 3x
direct: ∂f/∂x = 2xy = 6x2
through y: ∂f/∂y · dy/dx = x2 · 3 = 3x2
df/dx = 6x2 + 3x2 = 9x2
check: f = x2·3x = 3x3, which differentiates to 9x2

Common slip

Answering 6x2 — the direct channel only. This is exactly the mistake people make with the Slutsky equation later in the course, where a price change moves demand directly and also moves it by changing real income. Same structure, same missing term.

Try it: find df/dx for f = xy, where y = 4x2

12x2

Direct: y = 4x2. Through y: x · 8x = 8x2. Together, 12x2 — and substituting first gives f = 4x3, same answer.

4 · Level curves Exam group 4

Stay with the mountainside. A level curve is the set of points where f takes some fixed value — if f is altitude, it's a path around the hill that never goes up or down. These are the closed loops you see on a hiking map, and the map itself is called a contour map.

The two pictures are the same function. On the left you can see the height; on the right you can't, but you get the loops back by labelling them. Economics almost always draws the flat version, so it's worth being comfortable with the idea that a curve on a two-axis diagram is really a slice through something with a third dimension.

Here is the useful part. Walk along one of those loops and your altitude never changes — so df = 0. That one fact hands you the slope of the loop.

df = 0  →  fx dx + fy dy = 0  →  dydx = − fxfy

Worked example

f(x,y) = xαyβ
fx = αxα−1yβ     fy = βxαyβ−1
dydx = − αxα−1yββxαyβ−1 = −αyβx
the last step is the ratio move from Math Proficiency 1

Hold onto this one. In Lecture 2 we will do exactly this calculation for a function that measures satisfaction rather than altitude, and the slope you get will turn out to be one of the most important quantities in the course. You will have already derived it; all that changes is what the function is measuring.

Common slip

Losing the minus sign, or flipping the ratio to −fy/fx. Check it against the picture: the curves in the second figure slope downward, so the answer has to come out negative.

Try it: find dy/dx along a level curve of f = 4x2y3

−2y3x

fx = 8xy3 and fy = 12x2y2, so the ratio is −8xy3 / 12x2y2, which collapses to −2y/3x. Reduce the numbers — the exam options are always in lowest terms.
